# Municipal Water Treatment: Unit Processes and Distribution

## Source selection and catchment protection

The cheapest treatment is the treatment that is never needed, which is why source protection sits
ahead of every unit process in a rationally designed system. Surface water from rivers and reservoirs
is abundant but exposed: it carries turbidity that spikes with rainfall, seasonal algal blooms,
agricultural nitrate and pesticide runoff, and pathogens shed by livestock and wildlife. Groundwater
from confined aquifers arrives naturally filtered and microbiologically clean, and often needs little
more than disinfection, but it brings its own chemistry — dissolved iron and manganese that oxidise
into visible discolouration on contact with air, hardness from calcium and magnesium carbonates, and
in specific geologies naturally occurring arsenic or fluoride that are difficult and expensive to
remove. Catchment management, buffer strips along watercourses, restrictions on land use within
groundwater protection zones and control of septic discharge all reduce the load reaching the works.
Utilities that neglect the catchment end up paying for it permanently in chemical dose and in the
capital cost of processes they would not otherwise need.

## Coagulation, flocculation and clarification

Most of what makes surface water turbid is colloidal: particles small enough that gravity will never
settle them within a useful time, held in suspension because they carry mutually repelling negative
surface charges. Coagulation neutralises that charge. A metal salt — aluminium sulphate, polyaluminium
chloride or ferric chloride — is dosed and dispersed in a few seconds of violent mixing, and the
hydrolysing metal ions collapse the electrical double layer so particles can approach one another.
Flocculation follows in a slow-mixing basin over twenty to forty minutes, where gentle velocity
gradients bring destabilised particles into contact often enough to aggregate into visible floc
without shearing it apart again. The grown floc is then removed, classically in a sedimentation basin
sized for a few hours of residence, increasingly in dissolved air flotation where fine bubbles carry
low-density floc and algae upward to be skimmed. Coagulation is strongly pH dependent, with a narrow
optimum for each coagulant, and jar testing remains the standard method for finding the dose and pH
that a given raw water actually requires.

## Filtration and membrane processes

Clarification removes the bulk of the solids; filtration removes what is left. A rapid gravity filter
passes water downward through graded media — commonly a metre of anthracite over sand, or sand alone
— at a few metres per hour, capturing particles by physical straining near the surface and by
adhesion deeper within the bed. Head loss rises as the bed loads, and the filter is periodically
taken offline and backwashed with reversed flow, often with air scour, to fluidise the media and
carry accumulated solids to waste. The first minutes after a filter returns to service produce a
turbidity spike, and filter-to-waste during that ripening period is a standard safeguard because
Cryptosporidium oocysts are chlorine-resistant and physical removal is the only barrier against them.
Slow sand filtration works on a different principle entirely, relying on the biologically active
schmutzdecke at the surface, and it produces excellent water with minimal chemistry but demands a
large footprint. Membrane processes — microfiltration and ultrafiltration for particles and
pathogens, reverse osmosis for dissolved salts — offer an absolute physical barrier defined by pore
size rather than a statistical one, at higher energy cost and with a concentrate stream to dispose of.

## Disinfection, CT and by-products

Disinfection is where the public health guarantee is actually made, and its design currency is CT: the
product of disinfectant residual concentration and contact time, which must exceed a value tabulated
for the target organism, the temperature and the pH. Free chlorine is inexpensive, effective against
bacteria and viruses, and uniquely valuable because it persists into the distribution network. It is
almost useless against Cryptosporidium. Ozone and ultraviolet irradiation invert that profile: UV
inactivates protozoa efficiently at modest doses by damaging nucleic acid, but leaves no residual at
all. The trade-off that dominates modern practice is disinfection by-product formation. Chlorine
reacts with natural organic matter to form trihalomethanes and haloacetic acids, both regulated as
probable carcinogens, and the response is to remove the organic precursors before chlorination rather
than to under-dose the disinfectant. Chloramination, using a chlorine and ammonia combination,
produces a longer-lasting and lower-by-product residual and is widely used for distribution, though
it is a weaker primary disinfectant and brings nitrification risk in the network.

## Corrosion control and distribution network integrity

Water that leaves the works compliant can arrive at the tap unsafe, and the mechanism is usually the
pipe. Lead service lines and lead-tin solder release lead into water that is corrosive to them, and
because the release is a chemical function of the water rather than a contamination event, it is
controlled by conditioning the water instead of by treating the contamination. Orthophosphate dosing
forms a stable, insoluble lead phosphate scale on the pipe interior that acts as a physical barrier,
and maintaining pH in the region of 7.5 to 8.5 with adequate alkalinity keeps that scale intact. The
critical operational lesson, learned repeatedly and expensively, is that switching source water or
changing coagulant can destabilise decades-old protective scale and release accumulated lead in
weeks. Physical integrity matters equally: a distribution system must be kept under continuous
positive pressure, because a pressure loss during a main break can draw contaminated groundwater
through the same defect that is leaking, and any repair therefore requires disinfection and
bacteriological clearance before the main returns to service.

## Monitoring, residuals and the multi-barrier principle

Compliance monitoring divides into operational parameters measured continuously — turbidity, pH,
chlorine residual, flow — and compliance samples sent to a laboratory at defined frequencies. Turbidity
is the most informative single continuous measurement in the plant, because it is a real-time proxy
for filtration performance and therefore for pathogen removal, and individual filter turbidity
monitoring detects a failing filter that blended plant effluent would hide. Coliform testing detects
faecal contamination indirectly, since the indicator organisms are far easier to culture than the
pathogens they stand in for. Underlying all of it is the multi-barrier principle: no single process is
trusted absolutely, because every one has a failure mode, and the system is designed so that source
protection, coagulation, filtration, disinfection and distribution integrity each independently reduce
risk. Treatment also generates residuals — coagulant sludge, spent backwash water, membrane
concentrate — and their handling and disposal is a genuine part of the plant's design rather than an
afterthought bolted on at the end.
