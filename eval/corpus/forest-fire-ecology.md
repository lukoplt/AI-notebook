# Forest Fire Ecology: Regimes, Behaviour and Management

## Fire regimes and evolutionary adaptation

Fire is not an interruption of forest ecology; in most of the world's forests it is a component of it,
and the useful descriptor is the fire regime — the characteristic frequency, intensity, seasonality,
size and spatial pattern of burning in a given system. Regimes fall into broad classes. Frequent
low-intensity surface regimes, historically every five to twenty-five years in ponderosa pine and
many dry Mediterranean systems, consume litter and understorey while leaving mature trees alive.
Infrequent high-intensity crown regimes, at intervals of one to several centuries in boreal spruce and
lodgepole pine, kill entire stands and reset succession. Species carry adaptations matched to the
regime they evolved under, and the two classes produce opposite strategies. Thick insulating bark,
self-pruning of lower branches and elevated crowns are resistance traits for surface fire. Serotinous
cones sealed with resin that opens only under the heat of a crown fire, and heat-triggered or
smoke-triggered seed germination, are regeneration traits for stand-replacing fire. A frequent-fire
species subjected to a crown regime dies, and vice versa.

## The fire behaviour triangle and fuel structure

Fire behaviour is governed by three interacting factors: fuel, weather and topography. Fuel matters
not by total quantity but by arrangement. Fine fuels below about six millimetres in diameter — grass,
needles, small twigs — respond to atmospheric moisture within an hour and carry the flaming front;
heavy fuels dry over weeks and govern residual burning and soil heating, not spread. The vertical
arrangement determines whether a fire stays on the ground: ladder fuels such as saplings, low
branches and tall shrubs connect the surface to the canopy, and without them a surface fire cannot
usually reach the crowns. Weather supplies the short-term variability, with wind exerting the
strongest single control on spread rate and direction, and relative humidity setting fine fuel
moisture on an hourly basis. Topography adds a persistent bias: fire spreads far faster upslope,
because flames preheat the fuel above them by radiation and convection, and a twenty-degree slope can
roughly double the rate of spread relative to flat ground.

## Crown fire, spotting and extreme behaviour

The transition from surface to crown fire is a threshold event that changes everything about a fire's
speed, intensity and controllability. A passive crown fire torches individual trees as the fire
passes; an active crown fire propagates through the canopy as a coherent front, sustained by the
surface fire beneath it; an independent crown fire, rare and requiring extreme wind, runs through the
canopy ahead of any surface support. Spotting compounds the problem: convection columns loft burning
embers, and firebrands can start new ignitions hundreds of metres to several kilometres downwind,
seeding fires far beyond any control line. The most dangerous behaviours emerge when the fire begins
to drive its own weather. A sufficiently intense plume can form a pyrocumulonimbus cloud, which
generates downdrafts that hit the ground and spread outward in unpredictable directions, and can
produce dry lightning that starts fires elsewhere. Fires burning in adjacent drainages can merge with
sudden acceleration, and terrain features such as narrow canyons and saddles produce channelled
winds that have repeatedly killed experienced crews.

## Fire suppression and the fire deficit

A century of near-total suppression across North America and parts of Europe produced an outcome
directly opposite to its intent in frequent-fire forests. Excluding the low-intensity surface fires
that historically thinned understorey every decade or two allowed fuel to accumulate continuously and
allowed dense shade-tolerant regeneration to establish beneath old open-grown stands — building
precisely the ladder fuel structure that converts a manageable surface fire into an unstoppable crown
fire. The result is a fire deficit, a growing gap between the area that would have burned under the
historical regime and the area actually burning, discharged eventually as fires far more severe than
the system evolved with. The paradox is now well documented: successful suppression in the short run
guarantees worse fire in the long run, because it removes the small disturbances that prevented large
ones. This does not argue against suppression near communities; it argues that suppression alone is
not a management strategy, and that the fuel debt accumulates whether or not it is acknowledged.

## Prescribed burning and mechanical treatment

Deliberately reintroducing fire under chosen conditions is the most direct way to pay down the fuel
debt, and it is done within a prescription: a defined envelope of fuel moisture, wind speed and
direction, relative humidity, temperature and atmospheric mixing height within which the burn will
behave as intended. The mixing height matters as much as the fire behaviour parameters, because smoke
that does not disperse creates a public health problem that ends prescribed burning programmes
politically. Weather windows meeting all criteria are narrow and shrinking as fire seasons lengthen,
which is the practical constraint on scaling the work. Where burning is impossible — too close to
housing, too much accumulated fuel to burn safely on the first entry — mechanical thinning removes
ladder fuels and reduces canopy bulk density, but it leaves slash that must be piled and burned or
chipped, and it does not reproduce fire's effects on soil chemistry, seed germination or nutrient
cycling. The two are complementary: thinning first, then burning, is the standard sequence for
restoring a heavily encroached stand.

## Post-fire response, salvage and recovery

The most consequential post-fire process is hydrological rather than botanical. Fire removes the
canopy and litter that intercept rainfall and destroys the root structure that binds soil, and intense
heat can drive organic compounds into the soil profile where they condense into a water-repellent
hydrophobic layer. The result is that a burned catchment can generate several times the runoff of an
unburned one from the same rainfall, and the first significant storm after a fire — not the fire
itself — produces the debris flows that most often kill people and destroy infrastructure downstream.
Emergency stabilisation therefore prioritises drainage protection and early warning over replanting.
Ecological recovery is usually faster than public expectation, since fire-adapted systems regenerate
from serotinous seed, from soil seed banks and from resprouting root crowns, and standing dead trees
provide critical habitat for cavity-nesting birds and insects. Post-fire salvage logging is
contentious for this reason: it recovers economic value but compacts recovering soil, removes the
snags and coarse woody debris that structure early-successional habitat, and where it is followed by
dense uniform replanting it can set up the next stand to burn severely.
