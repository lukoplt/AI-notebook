# Volcanology: Magma, Eruption Style and Hazard Forecasting

## Magma composition and the role of silica

Everything an eruption does is downstream of the magma's silica content. Basaltic melt, at roughly
fifty percent silica by weight, has short, poorly polymerised silicate chains and a viscosity in the
range of hundreds of pascal-seconds at eruptive temperature, so it flows readily and lets gas bubbles
rise and escape. Rhyolitic melt, at seventy percent silica or more, forms long interlinked chains and
reaches viscosities millions of times higher, trapping gas until pressure overcomes the strength of
the melt. Andesite and dacite occupy the middle and produce the intermediate behaviour typical of
subduction-zone stratovolcanoes. Temperature modulates viscosity strongly, with basalt erupting
around 1,100 to 1,200 Celsius and rhyolite often below 850 Celsius, and dissolved water lowers
viscosity substantially by breaking silicate polymer chains. This is why the same chemical
composition can behave differently depending on how much water has already exsolved. The practical
consequence is that composition predicts hazard: basaltic systems threaten with lava flows and
lava fountains, silicic systems threaten with explosive columns and pyroclastic density currents.

## Volatile exsolution and fragmentation

Magma at depth holds water, carbon dioxide and sulphur dioxide in solution because pressure keeps
them dissolved. As magma ascends, pressure falls, solubility falls with it, and volatiles come out of
solution to form bubbles — the process called exsolution. Carbon dioxide, being far less soluble,
exsolves deep and early, which is why increases in the CO2 to SO2 ratio in a volcano's plume can
signal fresh magma arriving at depth long before anything is visible at the surface. Water exsolves
shallower and drives most of the explosive energy. In a low-viscosity basaltic system the bubbles
rise faster than the melt and simply escape, producing benign degassing or a fire-fountain. In a
viscous silicic system the bubbles cannot escape; they expand in place until the volume fraction of
gas exceeds roughly seventy to eighty percent and the melt tears apart. That instant is
fragmentation: the material changes from a liquid containing bubbles to a gas containing particles,
and the eruption becomes explosive.

## Eruption columns and pyroclastic density currents

An explosive eruption drives a jet of gas and fragments upward at velocities of one to several
hundred metres per second. In the lowest few kilometres, the gas-thrust region, momentum alone
carries the mixture. Above it, the column entrains and heats ambient air, and if that heating makes
the mixture less dense than the surrounding atmosphere the column becomes buoyant and rises
convectively, sometimes to twenty kilometres or more, until it reaches its level of neutral buoyancy
and spreads laterally as an umbrella cloud. If entrainment is insufficient — because the vent is too
wide, the mass flux too high, or the gas content too low — the mixture stays denser than air and the
column collapses. The collapsing material becomes a pyroclastic density current, a ground-hugging
avalanche of hot gas and particles moving at up to several hundred kilometres per hour at
temperatures of several hundred degrees. These currents are the dominant cause of death in explosive
eruptions; they are effectively unsurvivable within their run-out, they are only weakly deflected by
topography, and they can cross water.

## Monitoring: seismicity, deformation and gas

Forecasting rests on three independent data streams. Seismic networks distinguish volcano-tectonic
earthquakes, which are brittle rock failure as magma forces its way through the edifice, from
long-period and very-long-period events, which reflect pressure oscillation in fluid-filled cracks
and are more directly tied to magma movement. The transition from discrete long-period events into
continuous volcanic tremor frequently precedes eruption by hours to days. Ground deformation is
measured by GNSS receivers, tiltmeters and satellite interferometric radar, and an inflating edifice
indicates a pressurising reservoir; modelling the surface displacement field against a simple
pressure-source geometry yields an estimate of depth and volume change. Gas geochemistry closes the
picture, with sulphur dioxide flux measured by ultraviolet spectrometry and CO2/SO2 ratios by
multi-component analyser. No single stream is sufficient. The strongest forecasts come from
convergence: escalating tremor, accelerating inflation and rising SO2 flux arriving together.

## The Volcanic Explosivity Index and its limits

The Volcanic Explosivity Index, introduced in 1982, ranks eruptions from zero to eight on a
logarithmic scale built primarily on erupted tephra volume, with column height and eruption duration
as secondary criteria. A VEI 2 ejects on the order of a million cubic metres; each step multiplies the
volume roughly tenfold; a VEI 8 exceeds a thousand cubic kilometres. The index is useful for
comparing eruptions across the geological record, where volume is often the only recoverable
parameter, and for communicating scale to non-specialists. It is a poor proxy for hazard. It says
nothing about lahars, which kill thousands in eruptions of modest volume when hot material melts
snow and ice; nothing about the direction of a lateral blast; nothing about population exposure or
evacuation capacity. A VEI 3 eruption beneath a glaciated summit above a populated valley is far more
dangerous than a VEI 5 in an uninhabited region, and hazard assessment properly proceeds from
scenario mapping rather than from a single index number.

## Lahars, long-term hazard mapping and evacuation

A lahar is a fast-moving slurry of volcanic debris and water with the consistency of wet concrete,
and it is the most reliably lethal volcanic hazard because it can occur without any eruption at all —
heavy rain remobilising loose deposits is sufficient. Lahars follow valleys, which is precisely where
roads, farmland and towns concentrate, and they can travel tens of kilometres from the volcano while
retaining destructive energy. Because their paths are topographically controlled, they are the most
mappable of volcanic hazards, and inundation modelling combined with the geological record of past
deposits produces zonation reliable enough to underpin land-use law. Acoustic flow monitors placed in
drainages detect the ground vibration of an approaching lahar and can trigger sirens with enough lead
time for vertical evacuation to high ground. The recurring lesson of the last fifty years is that the
scientific forecast is rarely the binding constraint. Warnings issued into communities that have not
rehearsed a route, or that distrust the issuing authority, do not produce evacuation.
