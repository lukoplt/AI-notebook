# Coffee Roasting: Process, Chemistry and Quality Control

## Green bean intake and moisture management

Green coffee arrives at the roastery in jute bags, GrainPro liners or vacuum bricks, and the first
operation is never roasting — it is measurement. A moisture meter reading between 10.5 and 11.5
percent is the accepted comfort band for arabica held in a temperate warehouse. Below 9.5 percent
the seed has dried past the point where it can conduct heat evenly, and the roast develops a
hollow, papery character no profile adjustment can rescue. Above 12.5 percent the bag becomes
hospitable to mould, and the cup picks up the wet-cardboard note that graders call phenolic taint.
Water activity, measured on a chilled-mirror hygrometer, matters more than raw moisture content
because it describes the water that is chemically available rather than merely present. A reading of
0.55 aw is stable for a year in a climate-controlled room; a reading of 0.65 aw will lose its
sweetness in four months no matter how carefully the bags are stacked. Density, measured in grams
per litre, is the third intake number. Dense beans grown above 1,600 metres absorb heat slowly and
tolerate aggressive charge temperatures; soft, low-grown beans scorch if the drum is loaded hot.
Roasters who skip intake measurement end up blaming their profiles for problems that were baked
into the lot before it ever reached the hopper.

## The drying phase and charge temperature

The first third of a roast is a drying operation and nothing more. Beans enter the drum at whatever
charge temperature the operator has selected — typically between 190 and 210 Celsius for a
half-kilogram sample roaster — and the bean mass immediately pulls the environmental temperature
down in what is called the turning point, usually reached between sixty and ninety seconds. The
depth of that turning point is a direct function of charge mass relative to drum capacity. Loading a
twelve-kilogram drum with four kilograms produces a shallow turn and a fast, thin roast; loading it
with eleven kilograms produces a deep turn and a sluggish recovery that flattens acidity. During
drying the bean colour moves from grey-green to pale yellow, and the aroma shifts from grassy to
something like toasted bread or basmati rice. The operator is watching for an even, unbroken rise in
bean temperature; any flat spot here propagates forward and shows up as a baked, cereal-like cup.
Most defensible profiles spend forty to fifty percent of total roast time in drying, and the
transition to the next phase is marked by the yellowing point rather than by the clock.

## Maillard reactions and the browning phase

Once free water has largely left the bean, the Maillard cascade begins in earnest. Reducing sugars
condense with free amino acids to form Amadori compounds, which fragment and recombine into
hundreds of volatile species: pyrazines that read as nutty and roasted, furans that read as caramel
and sweet, thiols that read as savoury and meaty when overdeveloped. Simultaneously the Strecker
degradation converts amino acids into aldehydes that carry much of what tasters call malt and
chocolate. This is the phase in which a roaster is genuinely composing flavour rather than merely
removing water. The controlling variable is the rate of rise — the slope of bean temperature over
time — and the governing principle is that the slope should decline smoothly and never invert. A
rate of rise that climbs back up mid-browning produces a harsh, ashy edge; a rate of rise that
crashes to zero produces the flat, papery emptiness known as a baked roast. Browning typically
occupies thirty to thirty-five percent of the roast, and its length relative to development is the
single strongest lever on perceived sweetness.

## First crack and development time ratio

First crack is an audible, physical event: accumulated water vapour and carbon dioxide rupture the
bean's cellulose matrix with a sound like popcorn, the bean roughly doubles in volume, and the
endothermic demand of the fracture briefly suppresses the rate of rise. Everything after that
moment is development. The development time ratio — development seconds divided by total roast
seconds — is the number most roasteries log, and the useful band for filter coffee sits between
eighteen and twenty-two percent. Below fifteen percent the cup shows unresolved acidity and a
vegetal, grassy top note because the interior of the bean has not caught up with the surface. Above
twenty-five percent the origin character has been sanded off and every lot converges on generic
roast flavour. Espresso profiles run longer, often twenty-two to twenty-six percent, because the
higher extraction pressure of a nine-bar shot amplifies any underdeveloped sourness. The ratio is a
descriptive statistic, not a target to chase in isolation; two roasts with identical ratios can taste
entirely different if their browning slopes differ.

## Heat transfer, airflow and drum design

A drum roaster delivers heat three ways at once. Conduction moves energy from the hot steel drum
wall into beans that touch it, and it dominates early in the roast when the drum is hottest relative
to the charge. Convection moves energy through hot air drawn across the bean bed, and it dominates
later once the air stream has been established; it is also far gentler and more even, which is why
convection-heavy machines produce cleaner, brighter cups. Radiation from the drum wall and the
combustion chamber contributes a modest but non-trivial share, and it is the mechanism responsible
for surface scorching when a hot drum meets a small charge. Airflow is the operator's most
underused control: increasing it raises convective transfer while simultaneously stripping chaff and
volatile smoke from the drum, and a roaster who never touches the damper is effectively working
with one hand tied. Fluid-bed machines invert the ratio entirely, running almost pure convection,
which is why their profiles do not transfer to drum machines without substantial rework.

## Degassing, packaging and cupping protocol

Roasted coffee continues to emit carbon dioxide for weeks, and that emission is both a preservative
and a nuisance. Immediately after roasting the bean is saturated with CO2, which crowds out oxygen
and slows staling but also disrupts brewing by forming a foam barrier that repels water. Filter
coffee generally peaks between four and ten days after roast; espresso wants ten to twenty-one days
because the pressurised extraction needs the gas load to have subsided. One-way degassing valves let
CO2 escape without admitting oxygen, which is why valved bags can be sealed within an hour of
roasting. Quality control closes the loop through cupping, and the Specialty Coffee Association
protocol fixes the variables that would otherwise confound comparison: 8.25 grams of coffee per 150
millilitres of water, a grind coarser than filter, water at 93 Celsius, a four-minute steep, then
breaking the crust and skimming before evaluation at three descending temperature bands. Scoring
covers fragrance, flavour, aftertaste, acidity, body, balance, uniformity, clean cup, sweetness and
overall, and a lot scoring eighty or above is specialty by definition.
