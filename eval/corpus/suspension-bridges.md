# Suspension Bridges: Structural Behaviour and Construction Sequence

## The load path from deck to anchorage

A suspension bridge is best understood as a load path rather than as a shape. Traffic and self-weight
press down on the deck; the deck transfers that load into vertical hangers; the hangers pull down on
the main cables; the main cables carry the resulting tension over the tower saddles and down into
the anchorages; and the towers carry a nearly pure vertical compression into their foundations. Every
member is doing one thing well. The cables are in tension, which is the only condition under which
high-strength steel wire is worth its cost, and the towers are in compression, which is what masonry
and steel boxes handle efficiently. The anchorage is the least glamorous and most critical element:
it must resist a horizontal pull of tens of thousands of tonnes, either by sheer gravity mass or by
transferring the force into competent rock. Where an anchorage cannot be founded, engineers resort to
a self-anchored configuration in which the cable terminates in the deck itself, putting the deck into
compression and changing the erection sequence completely, since the deck must then be built before
the cable can be tensioned.

## Catenary geometry and the sag ratio

An unloaded cable hanging under its own weight forms a catenary; a cable loaded uniformly along the
horizontal, as it is when supporting a deck of constant weight, forms a parabola. Real main cables sit
between the two, close enough to parabolic that design proceeds on that basis. The governing
proportion is the sag ratio — the vertical sag at midspan divided by the span length — and it
usually falls between one in nine and one in eleven. The trade-off it encodes is direct: a deeper sag
reduces cable tension, because the horizontal component of tension is inversely proportional to sag,
which means less steel in the cable and smaller anchorage forces. But deeper sag demands taller
towers, and tower cost rises faster than linearly with height while also increasing the foundation
load. A shallower sag flattens the profile and shortens the towers but drives cable tension up
sharply, and at very shallow sags the cable's own stiffness contribution collapses and the structure
becomes uncomfortably flexible under moving load. The optimum is a cost minimum, not a structural
one.

## Aeroelastic instability and deck stiffening

The collapse of the first Tacoma Narrows bridge in November 1940 is the discipline's foundational
lesson, and it is routinely misdescribed as resonance with the wind. What actually occurred was
torsional flutter, a self-excited aeroelastic instability in which the deck's own twisting motion
alters the airflow so as to feed more energy into the next cycle of that same motion. The mechanism
requires no periodic forcing at all; it needs only a bluff cross section, a low torsional stiffness
and a wind speed above the critical flutter threshold. The response was a permanent change in deck
design. Modern long-span decks are either deep stiffening trusses, which are open enough that wind
passes through rather than driving the section, or shallow streamlined box girders shaped like an
aerofoil, which generate far less vortex shedding and possess high torsional rigidity because the
closed box is efficient in torsion. Confirmation is empirical: every major span is now tested as a
sectional model and often as a full aeroelastic model in a boundary-layer wind tunnel before
construction commits.

## Cable spinning and wire metallurgy

Main cables are not manufactured as single units; they are spun in place, wire by wire, from a
travelling wheel that carries a loop of wire across the span and back, laying down two wires per
pass. Each wire is typically five millimetres in diameter, galvanised, and drawn to a tensile
strength around 1,760 megapascals, which is roughly four times that of structural steel. Several
hundred wires are gathered into a strand, and dozens of strands into the finished cable, which is
then compacted by hydraulic presses into a dense circular section and wrapped in soft wire or a
protective sheath. The critical craft issue during spinning is that every wire must carry an equal
share of the load, which requires adjusting each wire to a common sag under a common temperature —
work usually done at night when the temperature is stable and the sun is not differentially heating
one side of the span. Prefabricated parallel wire strand, delivered on reels and hauled across as
complete units, has largely displaced aerial spinning on shorter spans, but for the longest crossings
spinning remains competitive.

## Erection sequence and the closure problem

The order of assembly is fixed by physics. Foundations and towers first, then a temporary catwalk
suspended between them, then the main cables, then the hangers, and only then the deck. Deck erection
is the interesting part, because the geometry of the bridge changes continuously as segments are
added. Each new deck segment loads the cable, the cable stretches and its sag increases, and the
positions of every previously placed segment shift. Erection therefore proceeds symmetrically outward
from the towers or from midspan, and every segment is placed to a calculated pre-camber that will be
correct only when the last segment is in place and the structure has settled into its final geometry.
Closure — joining the two advancing fronts — is scheduled for a narrow thermal window, because a
twenty Celsius temperature swing can change the length of a kilometre of steel cable by more than
two hundred millimetres. Getting the closure wrong locks permanent unintended stresses into the
finished structure.

## Inspection, corrosion and cable dehumidification

The main cable is the one element of a suspension bridge that cannot be replaced, which makes its
condition the governing question for the asset's whole life. Corrosion inside a cable proceeds
invisibly: water enters through cracks in the wrapping, collects in the interstices between wires,
and produces both general section loss and, more dangerously, hydrogen embrittlement that causes
brittle wire fracture with no prior deformation. Historically the only way to assess it was invasive
inspection, wedging the cable open at selected panel points, sampling wires and extrapolating
strength across the whole cross section — expensive, slow and statistically fragile. The modern
answer is dehumidification: dry air is injected into the cable at intervals and exhausted at others,
holding the internal relative humidity below roughly forty percent, the threshold beneath which
steel corrosion effectively stops. Acoustic monitoring complements it, with sensors distributed
along the cable listening for the characteristic high-frequency signature of an individual wire
breaking, giving operators a running count of failures rather than a decadal snapshot.
