# Beekeeping: Colony Management Through the Season

## Colony structure and the division of labour

A honey bee colony is a single reproductive unit distributed across tens of thousands of bodies. One
mated queen lays every egg; her daughters, sterile female workers, perform every other task; and a
few hundred male drones exist solely to mate with virgin queens from other colonies. Worker
behaviour follows an age-based schedule known as temporal polyethism. For the first three days after
emergence a worker cleans cells. From day three to about day eleven she is a nurse, secreting royal
jelly from hypopharyngeal glands to feed larvae. From roughly day twelve to day twenty she processes
incoming nectar, builds comb from wax secreted by abdominal glands, and guards the entrance. Only in
the final third of her six-week summer life does she forage. Winter bees are physiologically
different animals: reared in autumn with high vitellogenin reserves, they live four to six months and
their job is not foraging but surviving until spring. A beekeeper who treats a September brood cycle
as though it were a June one has already lost the following season.

## Spring build-up and swarm prevention

Swarming is reproduction at the colony level, not a failure of management, but it costs the
beekeeper the majority of the field force at precisely the moment the nectar flow begins. The
trigger is congestion combined with a declining concentration of queen mandibular pheromone as the
colony's population outruns the queen's capacity to distribute it. The first visible sign is the
construction of queen cups along the bottom edge of the frames; once those cups are charged with eggs
the colony has committed and reversal becomes difficult. Prevention works on space and on brood-nest
geometry. Adding supers before the bees feel crowded, reversing brood boxes so the cluster has room
to expand upward, and checkerboarding drawn comb with foundation all delay the trigger. Where the
colony has already begun cells, an artificial swarm — moving the queen and a few frames of brood to a
new box on the original stand, leaving the flying bees to raise a replacement — satisfies the
reproductive urge while keeping both halves of the population. Simply cutting queen cells out is the
least effective response and usually buys nine days.

## Nectar flow, supering and honey chemistry

Nectar arrives at the hive as a dilute sugar solution, typically between fifteen and forty percent
solids depending on the plant and the weather. Converting it into honey is a two-step process. First
the receiving bees add the enzyme invertase, which hydrolyses sucrose into glucose and fructose, and
glucose oxidase, which produces small quantities of hydrogen peroxide and gluconic acid and gives
honey much of its antimicrobial character. Second, the colony dehydrates the solution by fanning,
driving moisture content down to eighteen percent or below, at which point the sugar concentration is
high enough that osmotic pressure alone prevents fermentation. Cells are then capped with wax. A
beekeeper adds supers ahead of the flow, not during it, because bees will not draw comb in a box
they have not yet decided to occupy. Harvest timing keys on capping: a frame more than eighty
percent capped is safe, while an uncapped frame should be tested with a refractometer, since honey
extracted above twenty percent moisture will ferment in the jar.

## Varroa destructor and integrated pest management

Varroa destructor is an ectoparasitic mite that reproduces inside sealed brood cells, feeding on the
fat body of developing pupae and vectoring deformed wing virus and acute bee paralysis virus. It is
the proximate cause of most colony losses in the temperate world, and untreated colonies in regions
where it is established typically collapse within two to three years. Monitoring is non-negotiable
and must be quantitative: an alcohol wash or CO2 roll of three hundred bees, expressed as mites per
hundred bees, with a treatment threshold around three percent in late summer. Sticky-board drop
counts are less reliable but useful for trend. Treatments fall into distinct chemical families —
organic acids such as oxalic and formic, essential-oil derivatives such as thymol, and synthetic
pyrethroids — and rotating between families is essential because pyrethroid resistance is now
widespread. Oxalic acid by vaporisation is highly effective but only against phoretic mites, so it
works best during a broodless period, either in midwinter or after a deliberate caging of the queen.

## Overwintering and colony nutrition

A colony does not hibernate. It forms a thermoregulating cluster that contracts as temperature falls,
with the outer mantle bees insulating and the core bees shivering their flight muscles to maintain
roughly 20 Celsius at the periphery of the cluster and 34 Celsius wherever brood is present. This
metabolic work consumes stores continuously, and the winter failure mode is rarely cold itself but
starvation within centimetres of food: a tight cluster cannot break to cross an empty frame in deep
cold. Adequate stores in a temperate climate mean eighteen to twenty-five kilograms of capped honey
positioned above and beside the cluster, not at the far edge of the box. Ventilation matters as much
as insulation, because respiration and cluster metabolism generate water vapour that condenses on a
cold inner cover and drips back onto the bees; a small upper entrance or an insulated quilt board
prevents this. Autumn feeding, when necessary, uses a heavy two-to-one sugar syrup that the colony
can dehydrate and cap before cold weather arrives.

## Queen quality, requeening and record keeping

Queen performance declines measurably after the second season as her stored sperm depletes and her
laying rate falls, and colonies headed by ageing queens swarm more readily and build up more slowly.
Assessment is done on the brood pattern rather than on the queen herself: a solid, contiguous slab of
sealed worker brood with few skipped cells indicates a well-mated, healthy queen, while a spotty
pattern suggests poor mating, disease, or inbreeding depression. A colony rearing drone brood in
worker cells has lost its queen entirely and is being laid by workers, a condition that cannot be
corrected by simply introducing a new queen. Deliberate requeening every one to two years, using
stock selected for hygienic behaviour and mite resistance, is the most reliable long-term
intervention available to a beekeeper. All of it depends on records: date of inspection, queen
status, frames of brood, stores, mite count and treatment applied. Memory across twenty colonies and
six months is a fiction, and undocumented management is indistinguishable from guessing.
