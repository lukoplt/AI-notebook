# Sourdough Fermentation: Microbiology, Dough Handling and Baking

## The starter as a microbial ecosystem

A sourdough starter is a stable co-culture, not a single organism, and its stability is the product of
repeated selection rather than of any deliberate inoculation. Wild yeasts, most often
Kazachstania humilis or Saccharomyces cerevisiae, ferment sugars to carbon dioxide and ethanol and
provide the leavening. Lactic acid bacteria, predominantly heterofermentative species of the
Lactiplantibacillus and Fructilactobacillus genera, produce lactic and acetic acid and supply nearly
all of the flavour. The two groups coexist rather than compete because they partition the substrate:
the bacteria prefer maltose, which the yeasts largely ignore, while the yeasts consume glucose and
fructose. Bacterial cells outnumber yeast cells by roughly a hundred to one in a mature culture. The
acids they produce drive pH down to between 3.5 and 4.2, and that acidity is the ecosystem's own
defence, excluding the spoilage organisms and pathogens that cannot tolerate it. This is why a
neglected starter goes sour and sluggish but does not usually go dangerous, and why a starter is
maintained by refreshment rather than by sterility.

## Refreshment schedule, hydration and temperature control

Maintaining a starter means repeatedly diluting the culture with fresh flour and water before it
exhausts its substrate and acidifies to the point of self-inhibition. The controlling variables are
the inoculation ratio, the hydration and the temperature. A 1:5:5 refreshment — one part starter to
five parts each of flour and water — at 26 Celsius peaks in roughly six to eight hours; a 1:1:1
refreshment peaks in three or four; and a 1:20:20 stretches past twelve. Temperature shifts the
balance between the two microbial populations, not merely the speed of the whole. Warm fermentation
above 28 Celsius favours the bacteria and specifically lactic acid production, giving a mild, yoghurt
like tang. Cooler fermentation between 18 and 22 Celsius favours acetic acid, giving the sharper,
more vinegar-like edge. Stiff starters at fifty percent hydration also push toward acetic acid,
because limited water restricts diffusion, while slack, liquid starters run lactic. A baker who wants
to change the flavour of a bread should adjust the starter's conditions before adjusting anything in
the dough.

## Autolyse, gluten development and dough strength

Mixing flour with water and letting it stand before adding starter and salt is the autolyse, and it
does two useful things at once. Flour proteins hydrate fully, and endogenous protease and amylase
enzymes begin to work — the proteases relaxing the protein network so it extends more readily, the
amylases liberating fermentable sugars from damaged starch. Gluten itself is not present in flour; it
forms when glutenin and gliadin hydrate and are worked together, glutenin contributing elasticity
and gliadin contributing extensibility. Development can be achieved by intensive mechanical mixing or
by time and gentle folding, and in high-hydration sourdough the second route is preferred because
intensive mixing oxidises carotenoid pigments and strips flavour. A series of stretch-and-folds
spaced across the bulk ferment aligns the protein network, redistributes the gases and equalises
temperature through the mass. Dough strength is assessed by feel and by the windowpane test, in which
a small piece stretched thin should transmit light without tearing.

## Bulk fermentation and reading the dough

Bulk fermentation is where the loaf is actually made, and the single most common failure in home
sourdough is judging it by the clock. Fermentation rate roughly doubles for every eight to ten Celsius
of temperature rise, so a recipe timing written for a 24 Celsius kitchen is meaningless at 29. The
reliable indicators are volumetric and textural: a rise of fifty to seventy-five percent for a strong
bread flour, less for a weak or heavily wholegrain dough, together with a domed and slightly jiggly
surface, visible bubbles at the edge of the container, and a marked lightening of the mass in the
hand. Under-fermented dough tears rather than stretches, bakes dense, and produces a tight crumb with
a pale, gummy centre. Over-fermented dough loses structure as protease activity degrades the gluten
faster than it can be rebuilt; it spreads on the bench, fails to hold a shape, and bakes into a flat
loaf with a fragile, crumbly crumb. Over-fermentation is not recoverable by adding flour or by longer
proofing.

## Shaping, proofing and cold retardation

Shaping organises the fermented mass into a structure with an oriented surface tension that will
direct expansion in the oven. Pre-shaping into a loose round, resting on the bench for twenty to
thirty minutes to relax the gluten, then final shaping with a taut outer skin is the standard
sequence, and the taut skin is what allows a scored loaf to open cleanly rather than bursting at a
random weak point. The shaped loaf then proofs in a banneton, and here most bakers use cold
retardation: several hours to two days at 3 to 5 Celsius. Retardation slows yeast activity sharply
while the acid-producing bacteria continue working at a relatively higher rate, so the flavour
deepens and grows more complex without the loaf over-proofing. The cold also firms the dough, which
makes a slack, high-hydration loaf far easier to score cleanly. Readiness is judged by the poke test:
a lightly floured finger pressed into the surface should leave an indentation that springs back
slowly and incompletely.

## Oven spring, crust formation and staling

The first minutes in the oven produce oven spring, and three mechanisms drive it simultaneously: gas
already dissolved in the dough comes out of solution as it warms, existing bubbles expand according
to the gas laws, and the yeast enters a final burst of activity before heat kills it around 60
Celsius. Steam in the first phase of the bake is what allows that expansion to be realised, because it
keeps the surface moist and pliable and delays crust setting; a Dutch oven works by trapping the
loaf's own evaporated water. Once steam is removed the surface dehydrates, and the crust forms
through Maillard browning between amino acids and reducing sugars above roughly 140 Celsius, followed
by caramelisation of sugars at higher temperatures. Internally the loaf is finished when starch has
gelatinised and the crumb has set, around 96 to 99 Celsius at the centre. Cooling is part of baking:
cutting a hot loaf releases steam that would otherwise redistribute into the crumb, and it leaves a
gummy texture. Staling that follows over the next days is starch retrogradation, the recrystallisation
of amylopectin, not simple moisture loss — which is why a stale loaf briefly refreshes when reheated,
and why refrigeration accelerates staling while freezing arrests it.
